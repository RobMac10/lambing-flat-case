In front of a banner, Young, New South Wales

Painted 1861 · Still here

The banner is still here.

Someone painted it on a tent flap in 1861. ROLL UP. NO CHINESE. A cross, and stars, and those words, big enough to carry at the front of a march. And then someone kept it. And then a museum put it behind glass, as if it were only an object.

I stood in front of it and tried to imagine two or three thousand people walking behind that cloth, toward camps of men who had come here to dig. I tried to imagine the petitions that came after, written in English and in Chinese, asking to be paid for what was taken and smashed. I tried to imagine the courtroom where almost everyone walked free.

The banner survived. I’m not sure the people it was aimed at were meant to. Who started it, and did anyone pay? The file is still open. The cloth is still here.

The Lambing Flat riots, 1860–61

Who started it, and did anyone pay?

What is agreed

The Lambing Flat riots were a series of anti-Chinese attacks on the Burrangong goldfield, New South Wales, between November 1860 and July 1861. Lambing Flat was one part of the field. The main settlement later became the town of Young. The Riot Act was read on 27 January and on 14 July 1861.

The first anti-Chinese riot on this field is dated 13 November 1860. Further attacks followed, including 8–9 December 1860, 27 January 1861, and 19 February 1861. On 8–9 December, reports of two Chinese deaths were investigated by Superintendent Henry Zouch of the Mounted Police, who found that no deaths had occurred and that the reports had come from European miners.

The worst attack was on 30 June 1861. A crowd of European, American and Australasian-born miners, commonly put at 2,000 to 3,000, marched with a band and the “Roll Up – No Chinese” banner from Allandale (Tipperary Gully) to camps at Sawpit Gully and then Back Creek. Numbers of Chinese people at those camps are not agreed in the sources: one careful local account gives about 200–600 at Sawpit Gully and 400–1,000 at Back Creek; other accounts give different figures. Chinese petitioners later said they were beaten, that property was destroyed, and that one of them, Tom Me, had his hair cut off. One later synthesis says about 200 Chinese people were wounded, and that no one was killed on 30 June. This file records those claims, and does not invent a death toll the record has not settled.

The banner, painted on a tent flap, is now held at the Young Historical Museum. It shows a saltire and stars, with the words “Roll Up” and “No Chinese.”

Police arrested men identified as leaders. On the night of 14 July 1861 a crowd attacked the police camp; the police broke it up with gunfire and a mounted charge. One rioter, William Lupton, was killed. Troops, sailors and police reinforcements later arrived from Sydney and remained for a time. Premier Charles Cowper had already visited Burrangong in early March 1861, between the earlier riots and the June attack.

Most men tried for the riots were acquitted. Two were convicted: Claremont Owen, a watchmaker, sentenced to two years’ hard labour in Darlinghurst Gaol for his part in the 14 July riot; and William Spicer, a miner, sentenced to two years’ imprisonment for his part in the 30 June riot. Spicer was released on the Queen’s Birthday in 1862 after a petition of 2,989 signatures from the Lachlan goldfield. Some later retellings say only Spicer was convicted. This file follows the court record that both Owen and Spicer were sentenced.

Chinese miners and storekeepers petitioned the New South Wales government for compensation, in English and in Chinese. William Campbell, as a commissioner, examined claims. For losses on 30 June 1861 he reported 1,568 claimants asking a total of £40,623, and recommended that £4,240 be paid to 706 people. In September 1863 lists of approved claims were published in the NSW Government Gazette. In March 1864, 94 Chinese signatories at Tuena petitioned that their approved claims had still not been paid, almost three years after the riot. Whether the full amount recommended was ever paid remains an open question in the sources.

Later in 1861 the New South Wales Parliament passed the Chinese Immigrants Regulation and Restriction Act 1861 (often called the Chinese Immigration Restriction Act), which imposed a £10 tax on Chinese immigrants arriving by ship. The Gold Fields Act 1861 also allowed the restriction of “aliens” on the fields. Those laws sit in the same year as the riots. This file does not pretend the Parliament had only one reason for passing them.

Place and time

    Tom Me, What Young and Que You, and fellow petitioners

    Chinese miners and storekeepers · Petition after the 30 June 1861 riot · Names written in English and in Chinese on surviving petitions

    A voice from the people the banner was aimed at, written down in a claim for compensation, not in a newspaper crowd scene.

    Source. Petition of Tom Me, What Young and Que You, and others, for compensation for damages incurred on 30 June 1861, as quoted from the surviving petitions in the Museums of History NSW State Archives, and as published in later documentary work (including the Young Historical Museum’s account of the riots). A related earlier petition, of Hu Foo and Kylong and others, describes the attack of 19 February in the language of the Riot Act. Wording as quoted from those documentary sources. No period portrait of the named petitioners has been located. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness.

    What they said. Their petition stated that diggers and “other evil disposed persons” assembled “on foot and on horseback, to the number, at various times during the said day, of from eight hundred to three thousand, as your Petitioners believe,” armed “with bludgeons, fire-arms, and other weapons, and carrying banners or flags inscribed with the words “Roll up”, “No Chinese.”” They said the crowd “attacked your Petitioners and the rest of the Chinese,” beat them, and, “subjecting your Petitioner, Tom Me, to the indignity of cutting off his hair, drove your Petitioners and the rest of the Chinese, from the said diggings.”

    Could they really have seen it? They were there. They could describe what was done to them, and what was taken. A petition for money is still a sworn kind of public claim, written to a government, in a legal voice that borrowed the language of the Riot Act. They were not in the March meeting with the Premier, and they were not on the bench in the later trials. Their estimate of the crowd, “from eight hundred to three thousand,” already tells you they could not count it exactly.

    Why might they say it? To have the damage recognised, and to be paid. A petition can be exact about the cutting of Tom Me’s hair, and still be argued over when it comes to gold, tents and stores. Students can hold both things: this is one of the few places the record lets Chinese miners speak, and it is also a claim for compensation.

    Charles Cowper

    Premier of New South Wales · Visited Burrangong, March 1861 · Died 1875

    The head of the government that sent officials to the field, later saw men tried, and, in the same year as the worst riot, passed a law to restrict Chinese immigration.

    Source. Contemporary newspaper reports of Cowper’s visit, arriving on Saturday 2 March 1861, and of the later parliamentary passage of the Chinese Immigrants Regulation and Restriction Act 1861. This card does not invent a speech for him at Burrangong, and does not put quotation marks around words this file has not checked word for word. The portrait is a nineteenth-century image, Wikimedia Commons (File:Charlescowper.jpg), public domain. No likeness has been invented.

    What they said. Cowper went to Burrangong in early March 1861, after the summer riots and months before 30 June. The Miners’ Protective League had been formed in late January. This file does not invent what he said to the miners on that visit. What is not in doubt is the later record of his government: most rioters tried were acquitted; two were gaoled; and later in 1861 Parliament passed an Act imposing a £10 tax on Chinese immigrants arriving by ship, and a Gold Fields Act that allowed the restriction of “aliens.” In 1858 an earlier restriction bill had failed.

    Could they really have seen it? He could see the field in March, and he could see Parliament later in the year. He could not, from March, have seen the march of 30 June, or the attack on the police camp on 14 July. A Premier’s visit is not the same thing as standing in the Chinese camp when the banner arrived. He also cannot be made to stand in for the petitioners who were still waiting for payment in 1864.

    Why might they say it? To keep a goldfield from slipping out of the government’s hands, and to answer a political problem about Chinese immigration that had already divided his own side. Students can ask whether passing a restriction Act after a riot is a way of paying the rioters, or of preventing more violence, or both — and whether the people who were beaten were in the room when that was decided.

    William Campbell

    Commissioner examining compensation claims · Report, “Aggressions on Chinese,” 29 August 1862

    The official who sat between the petitions and the money, and who decided that some claims were genuine and some were not.

    Source. William Campbell, “Aggressions on Chinese, Compensation Claimed for Losses Sustained During Riots at Burrangong Gold Fields,” 29 August 1862, Votes and Proceedings of the Legislative Assembly of New South Wales, 1862, IV, pp. 9–27; and “Claims of Chinese to Compensation for Losses during Burrangong Riots,” NSW Government Gazette, 18 September 1863, pp. 2029–30. The 28 March 1864 Tuena petition of 94 signatories, stating that approved claims were still unpaid, is documented in later archival work on the Lands and Public Works files. No period portrait of this William Campbell is used here. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness.

    What they said. For earlier riots, in January and February 1861, Campbell examined 30 Chinese claimants seeking £6,339 and declared the destruction “very trifling, and that the claims herein before referred to are altogether fraudulent.” For 30 June 1861 he examined 1,568 claims totalling £40,623, recognised 706 as genuine, and recommended £4,240, after deductions. Approved names were later gazetted. In 1864, 94 Chinese signatories at Tuena said they had been approved and had still not been paid.

    Could they really have seen it? He could see the paperwork, and he could examine claimants. He was not an eyewitness to the march. A commissioner who calls one set of claims “altogether fraudulent” and then allows another set at about a tenth of what was asked is making a judgment, not taking a photograph. He also cannot, from 1862, tell you whether the gazetted sums were actually paid in 1863 or 1864.

    Why might they say it? To settle a government problem: how much, if anything, to pay. A report that cuts a claim heavily can be careful, or it can be hostile, or both. Students can set his “fraudulent” finding beside the 1864 petition from people whose claims he had already approved.

    Why it still matters

    The banner was kept. The petitions almost were not. Lambing Flat is still used, sometimes, as a colourful goldfields story, and sometimes as a starting-point for later White Australia laws. The file is open because a riot can have many hands, because a court can acquit almost everyone, and because a sum written in a report is not the same thing as money in a claimant’s hand. Who a nation decides to protect, after a crowd has already done the damage, is not only a question about 1861.

    Super Sleuths

    Young is on Wiradjuri Country. This file does not tell a Wiradjuri history of the goldfield, and does not invent one. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added.

    1. The notebook is written for this file

    The typographic page is a constructed student voice, standing in front of the Roll Up banner at the Young Historical Museum. The narrator is not a historical person. The opener does not invent 1861 dialogue. “Two or three thousand” is a range, not a head-count, and the agreed file says the numbers are disputed.

    Question. What is the difference between imagining yourself in front of a surviving object, and inventing a speech for the people who marched behind it?

    2. Who started it?

    There was not one riot and not one starter. A Vigilance Committee and, later, a Miners’ Protective League formed on the field. “Roll up” was a call to gather. Notices to quit were posted. A brass band led some of the marches. The 30 June crowd is often described as taking over a miners’ meeting. The record does not give you a single name at the front of the first November attack and still let you call the question settled.

    Question. If a file cannot name one starter, what finding is still honest — and what finding would be a guess?

    3. Numbers that do not agree

    Crowd estimates for 30 June run from the petitioners’ “eight hundred to three thousand” to the common later figure of 2,000–3,000. Numbers in the Chinese camps vary widely between accounts. Reports of two Chinese deaths in December 1860 were investigated and not upheld. A later synthesis says about 200 people were wounded on 30 June, and that no one was killed that day. William Lupton was killed on 14 July, in the attack on the police camp.

    Question. Which number in this file would you mark sure, which maybe, and which a guess — and what would you need in order to move a maybe up?

    4. One convicted man, or two?

    Some later retellings say only William Spicer was convicted. The court record used here is that Claremont Owen and William Spicer were both sentenced to two years, and that Spicer was released early after a large petition. Two unconvicted rioters are sometimes later said to have been elected to parliament. That last claim is not treated as agreed in this file without a named, checked source.

    Question. What happens to the question “did anyone pay?” if a later story quietly drops one of the two convicted men?

    5. Recommended is not the same as paid

    Campbell recommended £4,240 to 706 people out of £40,623 claimed by 1,568. Lists were gazetted in 1863. In March 1864, 94 signatories at Tuena said approved claims were still unpaid. Some later writers have doubted whether compensation was paid at all. This file leaves that question open. The earlier January–February claims he called “altogether fraudulent” are part of the same official story, and should not be hidden.

    Question. If you were writing a finding about payment, what would you need to see before you said “yes, they were paid”?

    6. The 1861 Act, and a longer shadow

    The Chinese Immigrants Regulation and Restriction Act 1861 imposed a £10 arrival tax. It is often treated as a direct result of Lambing Flat. The colony had already argued over a restriction bill in 1858. The riots, a petition from thousands of European miners, and the parliamentary debate all sit close together. Later writers have also drawn a line from this banner to the Immigration Restriction Act 1901. That line is an argument, not a fact the 1861 parchment itself can prove.

    Question. Is a new tax on Chinese arrivals a kind of payment to the rioters? What evidence would you use, and what would you still not know?

    7. The banner in the museum

    The 1861 banner survives at Young. The QR emblem in this file is a reconstruction of its wording and layout, not a photograph of the object. Keeping a banner can be memory, warning, or pride, depending on who is doing the keeping. Karen Schamberger and others have written about how this “difficult history” is shown in a local museum.

    Question. If a museum keeps the banner, what else has to be in the same room — the petitions, the acquittals, the unpaid claims — for the display to be a history, and not only a relic?

    8. Sure, maybe, or a guess?

    Sure: there were repeated anti-Chinese attacks at Burrangong in 1860–61; a banner with “Roll Up” and “No Chinese” was carried; most accused men were acquitted; Owen and Spicer were convicted; Chinese petitioners described beatings and the cutting of Tom Me’s hair; Campbell recommended £4,240; an 1861 Act put a £10 tax on Chinese arrivals by ship. Maybe: whether every gazetted pound was paid; exact camp numbers on 30 June. A guess: a single starter’s name, or the private thoughts of a man walking behind the banner. The opener is not a source for 1861.

    Question. Write a finding of no more than five sentences, and mark each sentence sure, maybe, or a guess.

    Teacher notes

    What’s true about the opener

    The typographic page is a constructed student notebook, written for this file, in the voice of a later visitor standing in front of the Roll Up banner at the Young Historical Museum. It is not an 1861 diary. It does not invent dialogue, and it does not treat “two or three thousand” as a precise count. The QR emblem is a reconstruction of the wording and layout of the 1861 banner, not a photograph of the museum object (even though a photograph of the banner circulates on Wikimedia Commons as public domain).

    Source conflicts, flagged

    Crowd and camp numbers for 30 June disagree. December 1860 death reports were investigated and not upheld; later writing still has to be watched for a creeping death toll. Some popular accounts say only Spicer was convicted; this file follows Owen and Spicer. Campbell recommended payment; a Gazette published names; an 1864 petition says approved money had not arrived; some later writers doubt payment was made. The official short title of the 1861 Act is given here as the Chinese Immigrants Regulation and Restriction Act 1861, also commonly called the Chinese Immigration Restriction Act; if a teacher has the enacted title in a slightly different form, this note should be corrected. Claims that unconvicted rioters later entered parliament are not treated as agreed without a checked source.

    Race, violence, and care

    This is a file about racist violence. The cutting of Tom Me’s hair was an assault and a humiliation, and is named in his petition. Beatings are named, not described for shock. Chinese names appear as they do in the English petitions, which are themselves transliterations; where Chinese characters survive on the archival petitions, teachers may wish to show that page. Living descendants deserve care. The banner’s language is quoted because it is the object, not because it should be treated as ordinary classroom talk.

    Young is on Wiradjuri Country. This file does not tell a Wiradjuri history of the goldfield and does not invent one. A deeper local treatment would need consultation. The student care line names Country; it does not pretend a consultation has already happened.
